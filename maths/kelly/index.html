The Kelly Criterion

published September 28, 2026

In 1956, John Larry Kelly Jr. was working at Bell Labs, and published A New Interpretation of Information Rate, a paper which applied the lessons of optimal transmission over noisy channels to the problem of gambling.

A game proceeds as follows. Every day at noon, you may make a bet for any amount $K\$ K$K of your choice (up to your total capital). A biased coin is tossed; if it lands on heads (which it does with probability ppp) you get $K×u\$ K \times u$K×u back, and otherwise if it lands on tails you lose your stake.

So the answer to “should we go all in” is in fact never unless p=1p = 1p=1 (when you are literally guaranteed to win).

It’s interesting to note that economists often assume [Kahneman & Deaton (2010) and Killingsworth (2021)] that people have utility functions which grow with the logarithm of income. This assumption traces back to Bernoulli’s 1738 attempt to resolve the St. Petersburg paradox.

Under this framework, utility maximisation is indeed log-wealth maximisation. But the Kelly criterion is totally independent of this! Kelly himself wrote as much in his original paper.

The reason has nothing to do with the value function which he attached to his money, but merely with the fact that it is the logarithm which is additive in repeated bets and to which the law of large numbers applies.

His criterion maximises the long-run median (typical) case, rather than going all in on positive bets, which amasses enormous expected wealth but concentrated in an exponentially unlikely single possible world. No other strategy can beat Kelly with probability greater than 50% in the long run, that is in the typical case [Bell & Cover (1980)]. And in fact, every strategy which is essentially different to Kelly has a vanishingly small fraction of Kelly’s almost surely [Breiman (1961)]!
