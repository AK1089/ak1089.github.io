Each prisoner can see everyone in front of them but nobody behind them (nor their own hat). From left to right, each prisoner must guess the colour of their own hat out loud. The prisoners may agree on a strategy beforehand, but cannot communicate once the hats are put on outside of their guesses.

How many prisoners can you guarantee guess correctly?

--- Let's see the solution...

Of course, random guessing only gets five correct guesses on average, with zero guarantees. But there is a way to get nine guaranteed correct guesses, using a simple strategy.

The first prisoner to guess sees nine hats, and counts the black ones. If this number is odd, he guesses 'black', otherwise he guesses 'white'. Of course, he might be wrong, but that's alright.

The next prisoner to guess sees eight of the rightmost nine hats. He can deduce the parity of the number of black hats in the rightmost nine from the first prisoner's guess. He can also count how many of the rightmost eight are black himself. This lets him deduce the colour of his own hat.

If we go back to the original puzzle with $k=2$, and identify white with $0$ and black with $1$, we get the strategy where each prisoner adds up all the hats he can see modulo $2$, and uses this sum and the previous prisoner's sum to deduce the colour of his own hat.

We can easily extend this to $k$ colours. Identify each with some number from $0$ to $k-1$ and add up everything you can see modulo $k$. The first prisoner guesses according to this sum (with probability $1/k$ of being correct), and every subsequent prisoner can guarantee a correct guess.

--- What if there are infinitely many prisoners?

## Infinite People

Suppose now that we are back to the two-colour case, but there is one prisoner for each natural number. Every prisoner now sees infinitely many people. They must have very good vision!

How many prisoners can you guarantee guess correctly now?

--- Let's see the solution...

Of course, the first prisoner might now see infinitely many black hats, which breaks the old strategy. But surprisingly, we can *still* guarantee that everyone except the first prisoner guesses correctly... as long as we use the [Axiom of Choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_choice).

Let's call two arrangements of hats *similar* if they differ in only finitely many places. This is an equivalence relation, which means it splits every possible arrangement of hats into infinitely many classes of similar arrangements. Within each of these classes, every pair of arrangements is similar. But every arrangement is dissimilar to every arrangement outside its class.

Before the hats go on, the prisoners choose one special arrangement from each class. Once the hats are on, each prisoner can see all but finitely many hats, and this is enough to determine which class the real arrangement belongs to.

So everyone now knows the class of the real arrangement, and thus the special arrangement in that class. And since they are in the same class, the true arrangement and the special arrangement are similar! There are only finitely many differences between the two.

Thus you can get finitely many wrong answers if everyone just guesses according to the special arrangement. But we are only allowed *one*.

But here we are counting something finite! The first prisoner counts how many of the hats he can see differ from the special arrangement: if this number is odd he guesses 'black', otherwise 'white'. Each later prisoner counts the differences in front of him, and knows exactly which hats behind him differ, because he has heard their (correct) guesses. The only difference is his own hat, so he can deduce whether it matches the special arrangement.

--- What? / But this is all still countable...

This is a bit confusing, so let's work through an example.

In the below example, the first 63 people have randomly coloured hats. But after that, every odd hat is black and every even hat is white. This arrangement is *similar* to the arrangement where every odd hat is black and every even hat is white the whole way down the list. In particular, there are only 26 differences (the crosses at the bottom). Scroll to the right to see!

Now, the first prisoner can see the infinitely many hats past #63 and knows that the real arrangement is similar to the alternating one. He counts all 26 differences between the two, so says 'white'. This is wrong, but that's alright.

The second prisoner now also recognises that the real arrangement is similar to the alternating one. He only counts 25 differences, so knows he must be one of the 26 the first prisoner saw. In the special arrangement he has a white hat, so for a difference to arise in this real arrangement his hat must be black instead.

Every further prisoner makes a similar deduction. From prisoner 64 onwards, they see no differences between their view and what it would be in the special arrangement, and they know the previous person saw an even number of differences too. So they know their situation matches the special arrangement, and can guess correctly.

--- But this is all still countable... / Take me back to the finite realm!

## Infinite Colours

Now, the same problem, but with infinitely many colours. Indeed, *uncountably many* colours. Say each hat has a real-numbered hue in $[0^\circ, \, 360^\circ)$. Can we guarantee at most one error?

Shockingly, the answer is still yes! We use the same strategy as above, where we fix a canonical special arrangement, and the first prisoner counts up the discrepancies between the special and real arrangement. Instead of counting, though, we have to add up all of the discrepancies modulo $360^\circ$. That is:

```math
\text{first prisoner's guess} = \mathrm{guess}_1 = \sum_{n=2}^\infty (\mathrm{hat}_n^{\text{real}} - \mathrm{hat}_n^{\text{canonical}}) \mod 360^\circ
```

While this looks like an infinite sum, remember that $\mathrm{hat}_n^{\text{real}}$ and $\mathrm{hat}_n^{\text{canonical}}$ differ in only finitely many places $n$, so almost all terms are zero and this is actually a finite sum in disguise.

Now, the second prisoner can guess as follows (again working modulo $360^\circ$):

```math
\mathrm{guess}_2 =
\underbrace{\mathrm{hat}_2^{\text{canonical}}}_{\text{known from the plan}} +
\underbrace{\mathrm{guess}_1}_{\text{just heard}} -
\underbrace{\sum_{n=3}^\infty (\mathrm{hat}_n^{\text{real}} - \mathrm{hat}_n^{\text{canonical}})}_{\text{can observe}}
= \mathrm{hat}_2^{\text{real}}
```

The guesses proceed like this, and every prisoner after the first guesses correctly.

You might object that summing modulo $360^\circ$ only works because hues happen to live on a circle. An arbitrary set of infinitely many colours has no natural way to add or subtract them, and then the first prisoner has nothing to announce.

But with the Axiom of Choice comes a theorem of [Tarski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski%27s_theorem_about_choice). This can be used to show that any infinite set of colours $C$ has as many finite subsets as it has elements. This gives us a group structure: take a bijection $f$ from $C$ to the set of finite subsets of $C$, pair each colour $c \in C$ with $f(c) \subseteq C$, and then define $c_1 * c_2 = f^{-1}(f(c_1) \triangle f(c_2))$.

The symmetric difference $\triangle$ makes the finite subsets a group (the empty set is zero, and every set is its own inverse), so this makes $C$ a group too. The strategy above then works with $*$ in place of addition and subtraction modulo $360^\circ$.

Of course, this requires the possible colours to form a set in the first place! So if each colour is indexed by an arbitrary ordinal, the Axiom of Choice is insufficient—we need the stronger [Axiom of *Global* Choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_global_choice).

## Deaf Prisoners

Now the same problem, except the prisoners can no longer hear each other's guesses. For all of these problems where we could guarantee all but one prisoner answering correctly, what is the best we can do now?

--- Let's see the solution...

In general, we can guarantee that all but finitely many prisoners answer correctly, just by everyone guessing according to the special canonical arrangement. By construction, this only makes finitely many errors!

To see why we can't do better, suppose we had a strategy guaranteeing that at most $N$ prisoners would ever answer incorrectly. Then fix any arrangement of hats on prisoners $N+2$ onwards. Prisoner $N+1$'s guess depends only on this, so give him a different colour. Then prisoner $N$'s guess is determined by all the hats he sees, so give him a different colour from his guess, and so on down to prisoner $1$. In this way, the first $N+1$ prisoners answer incorrectly, contradicting our assumption.

Of course, in the finite games, this means we cannot guarantee *any* correct answers.

--- I meant uncountably many *prisoners*! / Take me back to the finite realm!

## Uncountably Many Prisoners

Let's go bigger. Now there is a prisoner standing at *every real number*. Each prisoner can see everyone to his **left**, but nobody to his right (and, as always, not his own hat). The hats can be any colours you like, even uncountably many.

<div class="hats-diagram hats-past">
<svg class="responsive-svg" viewBox="0 0 640 206" role="img" aria-label="A band of hat colours along the real line, with one prisoner at time t who can see the colours to his left but not to his right. Drag to move him." xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"></svg>
</div>

Once again, the prisoners are deaf, and cannot hear any other guesses. How few mistakes can you guarantee?

--- Let's see the solution...

We can't hope for finitely many. Say the colour at $x$ is given by $f(x)$, with $f: \mathbb{R} \to C$. The prisoner at $x=1$'s guess is fixed given his view $f \upharpoonright (-\infty, 1)$, so give him a different colour hat. Then the prisoner at $2$'s view is also fixed, so do the same to him, and so on. Thus (countably) infinitely many prisoners could guess wrong, and this is unavoidable. But that's as bad as it gets! There's a strategy which guarantees only countably many mistakes, so the set of prisoners who guess wrong has measure zero—a randomly chosen prisoner guesses right with probability $1$.

Before the hats go on, the prisoners use the Axiom of Choice to put every possible arrangement of hats (element of $C^\mathbb{R}$) into a single well-ordered list, where every collection of arrangements has a first entry. Then, each prisoner looks to his left and finds the first arrangement in the list that agrees with everything he can see. He guesses whatever hat that arrangement gives him.

Why does this work? Call the set of wrong guessers $W$. This set is also well-ordered: every non-empty subset $A \subseteq W$ has a leftmost point. If it weren't, there'd be an infinite sequence $t_0 > t_1 > t_2 > \dots$ heading leftwards forever. If $G_i \subset C^\mathbb{R}$ is the set of arrangements consistent with what prisoner $t_i$ sees, and so $g_i = \min G_i$, then:

- Prisoner $t_1$ sees less than prisoner $t_0$, so more arrangements are consistent with his view: $G_0 \subseteq G_1$, and so $g_1 \leqslant g_0$.
- But $g_0$ agrees with the real hats everywhere left of $t_0$ (including at $t_1$) while $t_1$ guesses wrong. So $g_1 \neq g_0$, and so $g_1 < g_0$ *strictly*.

Repeating this, $g_0 \succ g_1 \succ g_2 \succ \dots$ get strictly earlier in the list forever. But such a well-ordered list has no infinite descending chains, so this is impossible.

So $W$ is a well-ordered set of reals. It is thus countable: between any two consecutive wrong guesses, there is a distinct rational number. So each wrong guesser gets a unique rational number, and there cannot be uncountably many.

Hardin and Taylor wrote about this theorem, describing it as [predicting the future](https://www.jstor.org/stable/27642415). If the real line represents time, and each hat is the state of the world, you can guess the present from the entire past and only be wrong countably many times—if you pick a moment at random, you are correct with probability $1$!

Of course, you can't actually use this. The well-ordered list can't be written down, and the theorem does not prove anything about any particular moment. But it's another strange consequence of the Axiom of Choice.

--- Take me back to the finite realm!

## Back To The Finite World

Let's return to the simple case where there are finitely many prisoners. To be precise, there are $N$ prisoners, and they are given hats of $N$ different colours. They can see everyone else's hat but not their own.

They must all guess at the same time. On average, one will guess right. But can you *guarantee* that at least one guesses right?

An interesting insight is that you can never do better than one hat correct on average, since each guess is correct with probability $1/N$ (as the guesses are independent of the wearer's hat colour). So to guarantee one correct guess, we need to guarantee *exactly* one correct guess, and so we can never have two people guess correctly.

One way to ensure this is by numbering the hats from $0$ to $N-1$, and having each prisoner guess that the total sum modulo $N$ is a different number. This means that they guess whatever colour of hat for them would make the total this number.

--- Let's make this harder for the prisoners!

## An Obstruction

What if not all prisoners can see each other? Let's take the case above, where $N=4$. Let's be generous and say there are only $3$ colours, so there is a bit more leeway. But let's put a wall in the middle, so no prisoner can see the one opposite them (only the one to their left and right).

Can you still guarantee that somebody guesses correctly?

--- Let's see the solution...

Surprisingly, yes! Number the colours $0$, $1$ and $2$, and let prisoner $i$ be wearing hat $h_i$ and guess $g_i$. Working modulo $3$, the prisoners guess as follows:

| Prisoner | Sees | Guesses |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $h_1$ and $h_3$ | $g_0 = h_1 + h_3$ |
| $1$ | $h_0$ and $h_2$ | $g_1 = h_2 - h_0$ |
| $2$ | $h_1$ and $h_3$ | $g_2 = h_3 - h_1$ |
| $3$ | $h_0$ and $h_2$ | $g_3 = -(h_0 + h_2)$ |

Opposite prisoners see the same two hats, so they can work as a team: one guesses a sum, and the other guesses a difference.

Why does this work? Let $e_i$ be how far off prisoner $i$ is: his real hat $h_i$ minus his guess $g_i$, modulo $3$. So prisoner $i$ guesses correctly exactly when $e_i = 0$. Writing these out,

```math
\begin{aligned}
e_0 &= h_0 - h_1 - h_3, & e_1 &= h_1 + h_0 - h_2, \\
e_2 &= h_2 + h_1 - h_3, & e_3 &= h_3 + h_0 + h_2.
\end{aligned}
```

Rearranging, we see that $e_2 = e_0 - e_1$ and $e_3 = -(e_0 + e_1)$.

Now suppose prisoners $0$ and $1$ both guess wrong, so $e_0$ and $e_1$ are each $1$ or $2$. Then either they are equal, so $e_2 = 0$ and prisoner $2$ is right, or they are different, so $e_0 + e_1 = 3 = 0$ and prisoner $3$ is right. Either way, somebody guesses correctly!

## Ebert's Hat Game

Let's take it down another notch, to three prisoners, each wearing a hat that is randomly black or white with equal probability, with no obstacles in play. This time, prisoners must guess their own hat colour in unison. However, prisoners are allowed to pass without making a guess.

The group wins if somebody guesses correctly and nobody guesses incorrectly. Equivalently, the group loses if someone guesses wrong or if everyone passes.

Can you guarantee a win? If not, what is the highest winning probability you can achieve?

--- Let's see the solution...

This time, you can't guarantee a win. But you *can* win $3/4$ of the time, using the following strategy. If you see your two fellow prisoners wearing the same colour hat, guess that you are wearing the opposite. Otherwise, pass.

Since your guess is always independent of your hat, the expected number of correct and incorrect guesses must be the same. Thus the optimal solution involves cramming all the incorrect guesses into as few overlapping worlds as possible, and spreading out the correct guesses into distinct worlds.

In the eight equally likely worlds, there are six correct guesses and six incorrect guesses made, circled below. But this leads to six winning worlds and two losing worlds!

Now, what about if passing gives you another chance? In this variation, if everyone decides to pass, you get another round: the game only ends when someone guesses.

--- Can you do better?

Yes! On round 1, the first prisoner guesses 'black' if and only if he sees two white hats (and there is a $50\%$ chance he is correct). On round 2, if the first prisoner passed, the second prisoner deduces that there is a black hat among him and the third prisoner. If he sees a white hat on the third prisoner, then he knows he must himself be wearing a black hat, and guesses accordingly. If not, then the third prisoner deduces on round 3 that he must be wearing a black hat to induce the second pass.

This gives you a $7/8$ chance of winning. See the eight possible scenarios below, and click on one to play through the scenario.

## Two Prisoners, Four Hats

Clearly, guessing $1$ is optimal in a vacuum, as you are right with probability $50\%$ (and thus they win with probability $25\%$). But they can do better! If they follow the strategy "guess the number you see", they end up winning with probability $1/16 + 1/4 + 1/16 = 3/8$.

This is weird, because if you see two black hats, your best guess is still $1$, not $2$. But if you guess $2$ and are right, your partner is *guaranteed* to be right. Reducing the probability that each prisoner's guess is correct can be worthwhile if it sufficiently correlates the two guesses.

--- But every prisoner is still wearing finitely many hats...

## Levine's Hat Problem

Let's extend this back to a beautiful infinite problem. Take the previous setup, but with two people wearing an infinitely tall stack of hats, each independently black or white with equal probability. Assume that they are not instantly crushed under the weight of these hats.

Simultaneously, each prisoner must name a number of some hat in *their own* stack. So if the left prisoner named $5$ and the right prisoner named $9$, that corresponds to the highlighted <span class="levine-colours">white and black</span> hats. The prisoners win if both named hats are black, so in this scenario they would <span class="levine-result">lose</span>.

--- But the stacks are uncorrelated?!

Once again, they do not gain any information from seeing the other player's hats. Individually, they cannot do better than probability $1/2$. But they can beat $1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4$ by correlating their guesses. For example, say they follow the strategy of naming the smallest-numbered black hat in their *opponent's* stack. Then the left prisoner would name <span class="levine-left-name">1</span> and the right prisoner would name <span class="levine-right-name">1</span>.

The number of the smallest black hat in each stack is a Geometric random variable with parameter $1/2$. Two independent copies of this distribution coincide with probability

```math
\sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}[X=n]^2 =
\sum_{n=1}^\infty (1/2^n)^2 =
\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{64} + \cdots =
\frac{1}{3}.
```

This beats $1/4$. Each individual player is still right with probability $1/2$, but they are *both* right more often, since their guesses are linked.

But is this optimal? In fact, no! There is a clever and subtle strategy found [here](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.4711) that achieves a $35\%$ success rate. But it's an open problem whether this is optimal. The best known upper bound is $81/224 \approx 36.2\%$.

## Further Reading

Hat puzzles go much deeper than this. Hardin and Taylor's book *The Mathematics of Coordinated Inference* covers many of the infinite versions here, and many more. And Levine is still open: closing the gap between $35\%$ and $81/224$ is a tricky problem indeed.
