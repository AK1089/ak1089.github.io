Black and White Hats

published October 31, 2026

You may well have heard the classic prisoner hat riddle. The setup is as follows: ten prisoners are led into a queue, facing the same direction as below. Each one is assigned a white or black hat by a guard.

Each prisoner can see everyone in front of them but nobody behind them (nor their own hat). From left to right, each prisoner must guess the colour of their own hat out loud. The prisoners may agree on a strategy beforehand, but cannot communicate once the hats are put on outside of their guesses.

How many prisoners can you guarantee guess correctly?

Of course, random guessing only gets five correct guesses on average, with zero guarantees. But there is a way to get nine guaranteed correct guesses, using a simple strategy.

The first prisoner to guess sees nine hats, and counts the black ones. If this number is odd, he guesses ‘black’, otherwise he guesses ‘white’. Of course, he might be wrong, but that’s alright.

The next prisoner to guess sees eight of the rightmost nine hats. He can deduce the parity of the number of black hats in the rightmost nine from the first prisoner’s guess. He can also count how many of the rightmost eight are black himself. This lets him deduce the colour of his own hat.

This continues down the queue. Each person sees an odd or even number of black hats, and knows which one the person behind them saw. The only difference between these is their own hat, so they can deduce its colour.

More Colours

Let’s mix it up. Now, every prisoner gets a hat coloured in one of kkk ways, with kkk known to everyone in advance. How many correct answers can you guarantee now?

In fact, the answer is still nine! If we go back to the original puzzle with k=2k=2k=2, and identify white with 000 and black with 111, we get the strategy where each prisoner adds up all the hats he can see modulo 222, and uses this sum and the previous prisoner’s sum to deduce the colour of his own hat.

We can easily extend this to kkk colours. Identify each with some number from 000 to k−1k-1k−1 and add up everything you can see modulo kkk. The first prisoner guesses according to this sum (with probability 1/k1/k1/k of being correct), and every subsequent prisoner can guarantee a correct guess.

Infinite People

Suppose now that we are back to the two-colour case, but there is one prisoner for each natural number. Every prisoner now sees infinitely many people. They must have very good vision!

How many prisoners can you guarantee guess correctly now?

Of course, the first prisoner might now see infinitely many black hats, which breaks the old strategy. But surprisingly, we can still guarantee that everyone except the first prisoner guesses correctly… as long as we use the Axiom of Choice.

Let’s call two arrangements of hats similar if they differ in only finitely many places. This is an equivalence relation, which means it splits every possible arrangement of hats into infinitely many classes of similar arrangements. Within each of these classes, every pair of arrangements is similar. But every arrangement is dissimilar to every arrangement outside its class.

Before the hats go on, the prisoners choose one special arrangement from each class. Once the hats are on, each prisoner can see all but finitely many hats, and this is enough to determine which class the real arrangement belongs to.

So everyone now knows the class of the real arrangement, and thus the special arrangement in that class. And since they are in the same class, the true arrangement and the special arrangement are similar! There are only finitely many differences between the two.

Thus you can get finitely many wrong answers if everyone just guesses according to the special arrangement. But we are only allowed one.

But here we are counting something finite! The first prisoner counts how many of the hats he can see differ from the special arrangement: if this number is odd he guesses ‘black’, otherwise ‘white’. Each later prisoner counts the differences in front of him, and knows exactly which hats behind him differ, because he has heard their (correct) guesses. The only difference is his own hat, so he can deduce whether it matches the special arrangement.

This is a bit confusing, so let’s work through an example.

In the below example, the first 63 people have randomly coloured hats. But after that, every odd hat is black and every even hat is white. This arrangement is similar to the arrangement where every odd hat is black and every even hat is white the whole way down the list. In particular, there are only 26 differences (the crosses at the bottom). Scroll to the right to see!

Now, the first prisoner can see the infinitely many hats past #63 and knows that the real arrangement is similar to the alternating one. He counts all 26 differences between the two, so says ‘white’. This is wrong, but that’s alright.

The second prisoner now also recognises that the real arrangement is similar to the alternating one. He only counts 25 differences, so knows he must be one of the 26 the first prisoner saw. In the special arrangement he has a white hat, so for a difference to arise in this real arrangement his hat must be black instead.

Every further prisoner makes a similar deduction. From prisoner 64 onwards, they see no differences between their view and what it would be in the special arrangement, and they know the previous person saw an even number of differences too. So they know their situation matches the special arrangement, and can guess correctly.

Infinite Colours

Now, the same problem, but with infinitely many colours. Indeed, uncountably many colours. Say each hat has a real-numbered hue in [0∘, 360∘)[0^\circ, \, 360^\circ)[0∘,360∘). Can we guarantee at most one error?

Shockingly, the answer is still yes! We use the same strategy as above, where we fix a canonical special arrangement, and the first prisoner counts up the discrepancies between the special and real arrangement. You might object that summing modulo 360∘360^\circ360∘ only works because hues happen to live on a circle. An arbitrary set of infinitely many colours has no natural way to add or subtract them, and then the first prisoner has nothing to announce.

But with the Axiom of Choice comes a theorem of Tarski. This can be used to show that any infinite set of colours CCC has as many finite subsets as it has elements. This gives us a group structure: take a bijection fff from CCC to the set of finite subsets of CCC, pair each colour c∈Cc \in Cc∈C with f(c)⊆Cf(c) \subseteq Cf(c)⊆C, and then define c1∗c2=f−1(f(c1)△f(c2))c_1 * c_2 = f^{-1}(f(c_1) \triangle f(c_2))c1​∗c2​=f−1(f(c1​)△f(c2​)).

The symmetric difference △\triangle△ makes the finite subsets a group (the empty set is zero, and every set is its own inverse), so this makes CCC a group too. The strategy above then works with ∗*∗ in place of addition and subtraction modulo 360∘360^\circ360∘.

Of course, this requires the possible colours to form a set in the first place! So if each colour is indexed by an arbitrary ordinal, the Axiom of Choice is insufficient—we need the stronger Axiom of Global Choice.

Deaf Prisoners

Now the same problem, except the prisoners can no longer hear each other’s guesses. For all of these problems where we could guarantee all but one prisoner answering correctly, what is the best we can do now?

In general, we can guarantee that all but finitely many prisoners answer correctly, just by everyone guessing according to the special canonical arrangement. By construction, this only makes finitely many errors!

To see why we can’t do better, suppose we had a strategy guaranteeing that at most NNN prisoners would ever answer incorrectly. Then fix any arrangement of hats on prisoners N+2N+2N+2 onwards. Prisoner N+1N+1N+1’s guess depends only on this, so give him a different colour. Then prisoner NNN’s guess is determined by all the hats he sees, so give him a different colour from his guess, and so on down to prisoner 111. In this way, the first N+1N+1N+1 prisoners answer incorrectly, contradicting our assumption.

Of course, in the finite games, this means we cannot guarantee any correct answers.

Uncountably Many Prisoners

Let’s go bigger. Now there is a prisoner standing at every real number. Each prisoner can see everyone to his left, but nobody to his right (and, as always, not his own hat). The hats can be any colours you like, even uncountably many.

Once again, the prisoners are deaf, and cannot hear any other guesses. How few mistakes can you guarantee?

We can’t hope for finitely many. Say the colour at xxx is given by f(x)f(x)f(x), with f:R→Cf: \mathbb{R} \to Cf:R→C. The prisoner at x=1x=1x=1’s guess is fixed given his view f↾(−∞,1)f \upharpoonright (-\infty, 1)f↾(−∞,1), so give him a different colour hat. Then the prisoner at 222’s view is also fixed, so do the same to him, and so on. Thus (countably) infinitely many prisoners could guess wrong, and this is unavoidable. But that’s as bad as it gets! There’s a strategy which guarantees only countably many mistakes, so the set of prisoners who guess wrong has measure zero—a randomly chosen prisoner guesses right with probability 111.

Before the hats go on, the prisoners use the Axiom of Choice to put every possible arrangement of hats (element of CRC^\mathbb{R}CR) into a single well-ordered list, where every collection of arrangements has a first entry. Then, each prisoner looks to his left and finds the first arrangement in the list that agrees with everything he can see. He guesses whatever hat that arrangement gives him.

Why does this work? Call the set of wrong guessers WWW. This set is also well-ordered: every non-empty subset A⊆WA \subseteq WA⊆W has a leftmost point. If it weren’t, there’d be an infinite sequence t0>t1>t2>…t_0 > t_1 > t_2 > \dotst0​>t1​>t2​>… heading leftwards forever. Repeating this, g0≻g1≻g2≻…g_0 \succ g_1 \succ g_2 \succ \dotsg0​≻g1​≻g2​≻… get strictly earlier in the list forever. But such a well-ordered list has no infinite descending chains, so this is impossible.

So WWW is a well-ordered set of reals. It is thus countable: between any two consecutive wrong guesses, there is a distinct rational number. So each wrong guesser gets a unique rational number, and there cannot be uncountably many.

Hardin and Taylor wrote about this theorem, describing it as predicting the future. If the real line represents time, and each hat is the state of the world, you can guess the present from the entire past and only be wrong countably many times—if you pick a moment at random, you are correct with probability 111!

Of course, you can’t actually use this. The well-ordered list can’t be written down, and the theorem does not prove anything about any particular moment. But it’s another strange consequence of the Axiom of Choice.

Back To The Finite World

Let’s return to the simple case where there are finitely many prisoners. To be precise, there are NNN prisoners, and they are given hats of NNN different colours. They can see everyone else’s hat but not their own.

They must all guess at the same time. On average, one will guess right. But can you guarantee that at least one guesses right?

6

An interesting insight is that you can never do better than one hat correct on average, since each guess is correct with probability 1/N1/N1/N (as the guesses are independent of the wearer’s hat colour). So to guarantee one correct guess, we need to guarantee exactly one correct guess, and so we can never have two people guess correctly.

One way to ensure this is by numbering the hats from 000 to N−1N-1N−1, and having each prisoner guess that the total sum modulo NNN is a different number. This means that they guess whatever colour of hat for them would make the total this number.

Ebert’s Hat Game

Let’s take it down another notch, to three prisoners, each wearing a hat that is randomly black or white with equal probability, with no obstacles in play. This time, prisoners must guess their own hat colour in unison. However, prisoners are allowed to pass without making a guess.

The group wins if somebody guesses correctly and nobody guesses incorrectly. Equivalently, the group loses if someone guesses wrong or if everyone passes.

Can you guarantee a win? If not, what is the highest winning probability you can achieve?

This time, you can’t guarantee a win. But you can win 3/43/43/4 of the time, using the following strategy. If you see your two fellow prisoners wearing the same colour hat, guess that you are wearing the opposite. Otherwise, pass.

Since your guess is always independent of your hat, the expected number of correct and incorrect guesses must be the same. Thus the optimal solution involves cramming all the incorrect guesses into as few overlapping worlds as possible, and spreading out the correct guesses into distinct worlds.

In the eight equally likely worlds, there are six correct guesses and six incorrect guesses made, circled below. But this leads to six winning worlds and two losing worlds!

Now, what about if passing gives you another chance? In this variation, if everyone decides to pass, you get another round: the game only ends when someone guesses.

Yes! On round 1, the first prisoner guesses ‘black’ if and only if he sees two white hats (and there is a 50%50\%50% chance he is correct). On round 2, if the first prisoner passed, the second prisoner deduces that there is a black hat among him and the third prisoner. If he sees a white hat on the third prisoner, then he knows he must himself be wearing a black hat, and guesses accordingly. If not, then the third prisoner deduces on round 3 that he must be wearing a black hat to induce the second pass.

This gives you a 7/87/87/8 chance of winning. See the eight possible scenarios below, and click on one to play through the scenario.

Two Prisoners, Four Hats

Now, suppose we have two prisoners. Each of them now wears two hats, each white or black with equal probability. Each can see the other’s hats but not their own. Simultaneously, they must guess the number of black hats upon their own head (from 000, 111, or 222). Both must be correct.

Clearly, guessing 111 is optimal in a vacuum, as you are right with probability 50%50\%50% (and thus they win with probability 25%25\%25%). But they can do better! If they follow the strategy “guess the number you see”, they end up winning with probability 1/16+1/4+1/16=3/81/16 + 1/4 + 1/16 = 3/81/16+1/4+1/16=3/8.

This is weird, because if you see two black hats, your best guess is still 111, not 222. But if you guess 222 and are right, your partner is guaranteed to be right. Reducing the probability that each prisoner’s guess is correct can be worthwhile if it sufficiently correlates the two guesses.

Levine’s Hat Problem

Let’s extend this back to a beautiful infinite problem. Take the previous setup, but with two people wearing an infinitely tall stack of hats, each independently black or white with equal probability. Assume that they are not instantly crushed under the weight of these hats.

Simultaneously, each prisoner must name a number of some hat in their own stack. So if the left prisoner named 555 and the right prisoner named 999, that corresponds to the highlighted white and black hats. The prisoners win if both named hats are black, so in this scenario they would lose.

Once again, they do not gain any information from seeing the other player’s hats. Individually, they cannot do better than probability 1/21/21/2. But they can beat 1/2×1/2=1/41/2 \times 1/2 = 1/41/2×1/2=1/4 by correlating their guesses. For example, say they follow the strategy of naming the smallest-numbered black hat in their opponent’s stack. Then the left prisoner would name 1 and the right prisoner would name 1.

The number of the smallest black hat in each stack is a Geometric random variable with parameter 1/21/21/2. Two independent copies of this distribution coincide with probability

∑n=1∞P[X=n]2=∑n=1∞(1/2n)2=14+116+164+⋯=13. \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathbb{P}[X=n]^2 = \sum_{n=1}^\infty (1/2^n)^2 = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{64} + \cdots = \frac{1}{3}. n=1∑∞​P[X=n]2=n=1∑∞​(1/2n)2=41​+161​+641​+⋯=31​.

This beats 1/41/41/4. Each individual player is still right with probability 1/21/21/2, but they are both right more often, since their guesses are linked.

But is this optimal? In fact, no! There is a clever and subtle strategy found here that achieves a 35%35\%35% success rate. But it’s an open problem whether this is optimal. The best known upper bound is 81/224≈36.2%81/224 \approx 36.2\%81/224≈36.2%.
